# CURIOUS REALITY — QUESTION ARCHIVE

## STATUS

Document Type: Question Registry, Curiosity Database & Future Content Pipeline
Brand: Curious Reality
Authority: Derived from CURIOUS_REALITY_MASTER_ARCHITECTURE.md

Purpose:

Maintain a permanent archive of questions that can become future Curious Reality:

- Shorts;
- long-form videos;
- documentaries;
- website articles;
- visual explanations;
- follow-up videos;
- research projects;
- knowledge graph nodes.

The Question Archive is one of the most important long-term assets of Curious Reality.

The brand follows QUESTIONS, not categories.

---

# 1. CORE PRINCIPLE

Curious Reality begins with questions.

The primary content transformation is:

QUESTION
→ RESEARCH
→ VERIFICATION
→ UNDERSTANDING
→ EXPLANATION
→ CONTENT
→ KNOWLEDGE

Therefore, the Question Archive should exist independently of the current publishing schedule.

A question does not need to become a video immediately.

A strong question may remain in the archive until:

- evidence becomes available;
- the right angle is discovered;
- the right visual treatment becomes possible;
- a related topic appears;
- a long-form project becomes appropriate;
- or the production system has capacity.

---

# 2. QUESTION AS THE FUNDAMENTAL UNIT

A Question is more fundamental than a video.

One question may generate:

- one Short;
- multiple Shorts;
- one long-form video;
- one article;
- multiple follow-ups;
- a visual diagram;
- multiple related questions;
- a topic cluster.

Therefore:

ONE QUESTION
≠
ONE VIDEO

---

# 3. QUESTION ID

Every important archived question should have a stable internal ID.

Recommended format:

```text
question-0001
question-0002
question-0003
```

The ID must remain stable after creation.

Do not use the final video title as the permanent question identifier.

---

---

# 5. QUESTION STATUS SYSTEM

Suggested states:

```text
CAPTURED
SCREENING
SHORTLISTED
RESEARCHING
VERIFIED
SCRIPTED
IN_PRODUCTION
PUBLISHED
EXPANDED
ARCHIVED
REJECTED
```

Meaning:

CAPTURED:
Question saved but not yet evaluated.

SCREENING:
Question currently being evaluated.

SHORTLISTED:
Question judged promising.

RESEARCHING:
Research has started.

VERIFIED:
The factual foundation has been sufficiently established.

SCRIPTED:
A content script has been created.

IN_PRODUCTION:
Visual, audio, or editing work is active.

PUBLISHED:
Question has become published content.

EXPANDED:
Question has generated additional content.

ARCHIVED:
Question and its resulting knowledge are organized for reuse.

REJECTED:
Question intentionally removed from the active pipeline.

# 8. EVERYDAY OBSERVATION QUESTIONS

Everyday observations are a high-value source of Curious Reality questions.

Examples:

Why is this object shaped this way?

Why does this happen every time?

Where does this disappear to?

Why does this feel different?

Why does this machine behave like that?

These questions often contain hidden mechanisms.

---

# 9. VIEWER QUESTION SYSTEM

Audience questions should feed directly into the archive.

Process:

VIEWER
→ QUESTION
→ ARCHIVE
→ SCREENING
→ RESEARCH
→ VIDEO

Useful comments may reveal:

- misunderstandings;
- deeper curiosity;
- missing explanations;
- requests for examples;
- requests for follow-ups;
- corrections.

Do not manufacture viewer questions.

---

# 10. QUESTION QUALITY TEST

A question is stronger when it has:

- clear curiosity;
- meaningful knowledge payoff;
- unexpected reality;
- evidence potential;
- visual potential;
- audience relevance;
- originality.

A question is weaker when:

- the answer is obvious;
- the answer is easily searchable but not interesting;
- no meaningful explanation exists;
- the topic is generic trivia;
- evidence is extremely weak;
- it duplicates existing Curious Reality content.

---

# 11. CURIOSITY TEST

Ask:

Would a curious person genuinely want to know this?

Does the question create an information gap?

Does the answer differ from common intuition?

Does the explanation reveal something hidden?

Would the viewer likely think:

"I never thought about that."

If not, reframe or reject the question.

---

# 12. KNOWLEDGE PAYOFF TEST

Ask:

What will the viewer understand after this?

A strong question should lead to a meaningful answer.

Possible payoffs:

- hidden mechanism;
- unexpected cause;
- common misconception corrected;
- surprising relationship;
- historical explanation;
- technical process;
- scientific principle;
- system-level understanding.

Avoid questions whose only payoff is:

"That's a random fact."

---

# 13. VISUAL POTENTIAL TEST

Ask:

Can the question be shown?

Possible visual approaches:

- real footage;
- macro imagery;
- simulation;
- diagram;
- animation;
- 3D reconstruction;
- comparison;
- scale transformation;
- cross-section;
- timeline;
- screen visualization;
- conceptual imagery.

A question does not need spectacular visuals.

But visual clarity should be possible.

---

# 14. EVIDENCE POTENTIAL TEST

Ask:

Can the answer be responsibly researched?

Check whether there are:

- primary sources;
- official documentation;
- research papers;
- datasets;
- authoritative references;
- strong reporting.

If the answer depends mainly on unsupported claims, classify it appropriately or reject it.

---

# 15. ORIGINALITY TEST

Before approving a question:

Search the existing archive.

Check:

- exact question;
- similar wording;
- same mechanism;
- same misconception;
- same topic cluster;
- existing videos;
- follow-up relationships.

If already covered, determine whether the new question adds meaningful value.

---

# 16. DUPLICATE QUESTION RULE

Do not create duplicate questions merely because wording differs.

Example:

```text
How does Wi-Fi work?

How does wireless internet work?

How does WiFi send data?
```

These may represent one broader question cluster.

Normalize them when they refer to the same underlying question.

Only separate them when they produce meaningfully different content.

---

# 17. QUESTION CLUSTER

Related questions should be grouped.

Example:

```text
CLUSTER:
How does a website load?

    ↓
Where does the request go?

    ↓
How does DNS find the server?

    ↓
How does the server respond?

    ↓
How does the browser render the page?
```

One cluster can generate an entire content series.

---

# 18. QUESTION FAMILY

A Question Family contains related questions around one concept.

Example:

```text
FAMILY:
Battery

Why does a battery store energy?

Why does battery capacity decrease?

Why does charging create heat?

Why does cold affect battery performance?

Why do different batteries behave differently?
```

Question families should be preserved for future research.

---

# 19. QUESTION DEPTH

A question can exist at multiple levels.

LEVEL 1 — OBSERVATION

What do we notice?

LEVEL 2 — SIMPLE REASON

What is the immediate explanation?

LEVEL 3 — MECHANISM

How does it actually work?

LEVEL 4 — DEEP STRUCTURE

What is happening underneath?

LEVEL 5 — CONNECTION

How does it relate to another system?

LEVEL 6 — IMPLICATION

Why does the result matter?

This allows one question to produce content at different depths.

---

# 20. QUESTION PRIORITY

Question priority may consider:

```text
curiosity
knowledge_payoff
evidence_quality
visual_potential
audience_relevance
originality
evergreen_value
timeliness
production_complexity
```

No single factor should automatically determine priority.

A simple priority classification can be:

```text
HIGH
MEDIUM
LOW
PARKED
```

---

# 21. HIGH-PRIORITY QUESTION

Typically contains:

- strong curiosity;
- clear payoff;
- reliable research potential;
- strong visual potential;
- meaningful audience relevance;
- little existing Curious Reality coverage.

These questions should enter the production pipeline sooner.

---

# 22. MEDIUM-PRIORITY QUESTION

Promising but may need:

- stronger framing;
- more evidence;
- better visual treatment;
- more context;
- better timing.

Keep active in the archive.

---

# 23. LOW-PRIORITY QUESTION

May be valid but currently has:

- weak payoff;
- low relevance;
- poor visual options;
- excessive production complexity;
- stronger competing questions.

Do not delete automatically.

Keep available for later review.

---

# 24. PARKED QUESTION

A parked question is potentially valuable but cannot currently be developed efficiently.

Reasons may include:

- evidence unavailable;
- technology not mature;
- event not sufficiently developed;
- visual resources unavailable;
- larger related project not ready.

A parked question should have a reason.

---

# 25. REJECTED QUESTION

Reject questions that are:

- misleading;
- unsupported;
- purely generic;
- duplicates;
- impossible to explain responsibly;
- based on fabricated premises;
- primarily created for clickbait;
- unrelated to Curious Reality's identity.

When practical, record:

```text
rejection_reason
rejected_date
```

---

# 26. TIME SENSITIVITY

Classify questions when useful:

```text
EVERGREEN
SEASONAL
TIMELY
CURRENT
EXPIRING
HISTORICAL
```

Examples:

Evergreen:
How does a refrigerator cool food?

Current:
What does this new technology actually do?

Historical:
Why did this historical system collapse?

Time-sensitive questions should be reviewed more frequently.

---

# 27. EVERGREEN QUESTION

Evergreen questions can continue to generate value for years.

Examples:

- how familiar systems work;
- why ordinary phenomena happen;
- foundational science;
- technology mechanisms;
- enduring historical questions;
- human perception.

Evergreen questions should form a significant part of the long-term archive.

---

# 28. TREND-BASED QUESTION

A trend can produce a question when there is something genuinely worth understanding.

Example:

Trend
→ "What is happening?"

or:

Trend
→ "How does this actually work?"

Do not create a video simply because a subject is trending.

The question must carry the content.

---

# 29. CURRENT-EVENT QUESTION

Current-event questions should be:

- fact-based;
- appropriately sourced;
- current;
- contextualized;
- clearly distinguished from speculation.

Current events should not turn the channel into a general news feed.

The question should reveal understanding beneath the event.

---

# 30. SCIENCE QUESTION RULE

Science questions must distinguish:

Observation
Research finding
Established explanation
Interpretation
Hypothesis
Speculation

The archive should preserve these distinctions when relevant.

---

# 31. TECHNOLOGY QUESTION RULE

Technology questions should identify:

- system;
- mechanism;
- component;
- dependency;
- limitation;
- real-world use.

Avoid surface-level "feature list" content.

Prefer:

How does this actually work?

---

# 32. HISTORY QUESTION RULE

Historical questions should preserve:

- time;
- place;
- participants;
- evidence;
- context;
- competing interpretation where relevant.

Avoid reducing complex historical processes to one unsupported cause.

---

# 33. PSYCHOLOGY QUESTION RULE

Psychology questions should avoid universal claims unless evidence supports them.

Prefer:

Why does research suggest this behavior can happen?

over:

Why do all humans behave this way?

Maintain appropriate nuance.

---

# 34. HEALTH QUESTION RULE

Health-related questions require stronger evidence standards.

The archive should record:

- evidence quality;
- uncertainty;
- source type;
- date;
- relevant limitations.

Do not treat anecdotal experiences as equivalent to scientific evidence.

---

# 35. LAW QUESTION RULE

Legal questions should identify jurisdiction and time period.

Example:

```text
Jurisdiction:
United States

Date:
2026
```

Do not assume one jurisdiction's legal rule applies globally.

---

# 36. POLITICS QUESTION RULE

Political questions should distinguish:

- documented fact;
- public statement;
- allegation;
- interpretation;
- opinion;
- prediction.

The archive should preserve neutral factual framing where possible.

---

# 37. MYSTERY QUESTION RULE

A mystery question must preserve the distinction between:

KNOWN

and:

UNKNOWN.

The existence of uncertainty is itself valuable information.

Do not manufacture a solution merely because the audience expects a conclusion.

---

# 39. QUESTION → ARTICLE RELATIONSHIP

```text
QUESTION

→ ARTICLE
```

Record the article relationship.

---

# 40. QUESTION → SOURCE RELATIONSHIP

Where a source directly helps answer the question:

```text
QUESTION
→ SUPPORTED_BY
→ SOURCE
```

However, sources should support the actual claims, not simply the general subject.

---

# 41. QUESTION → TOPIC RELATIONSHIP

Each question should have one primary topic where useful.

Example:


---

# 42. QUESTION → CONCEPT RELATIONSHIP

Questions may involve multiple concepts.

Example:

```text
Question:

Concepts:
Electrical Resistance
Chemical Energy
Heat
Battery Charging
Power
```

These relationships can feed the Knowledge Graph.

---

# 43. QUESTION → MECHANISM RELATIONSHIP

Where the mechanism becomes known:

```text
QUESTION
→ EXPLAINED_BY
→ MECHANISM
```

The mechanism is the underlying process that explains the observed result.

---

# 44. QUESTION → MISCONCEPTION RELATIONSHIP

A question may exist because people commonly misunderstand the subject.

Example:

```text
QUESTION
→ CORRECTS
→ MISCONCEPTION
```

This creates an additional content opportunity.

---

# 45. QUESTION → FOLLOW-UP RELATIONSHIP

A question may create another question:

```text
QUESTION A
→ CREATES
→ QUESTION B
```

Example:

```text
How does a battery work?

→ Why does the battery degrade?
```

Follow-up questions should provide meaningful new value.

---

# 46. QUESTION → DEEPER QUESTION

A simple question can lead to a deeper one.

Example:

```text
Why does metal conduct electricity?

↓ deeper

What happens to electrons inside the metal?

↓ deeper

Why does the atomic structure permit this behavior?
```

This allows curiosity to become structured learning.

---

# 47. QUESTION → COMPARISON

Some questions naturally create comparisons.

Example:

```text
Why does lithium-ion battery X behave differently from battery Y?

```

Comparison questions should identify the actual variable causing the difference.

Avoid arbitrary comparisons.

---

# 48. QUESTION → WHAT-IF

What-if questions can test understanding.

Examples:

What happens if gravity suddenly changes?

What happens if the internet routing system fails?

What happens if one critical component disappears?

What happens if temperature doubles?

These questions must be clearly framed as hypothetical where appropriate.

---

# 49. QUESTION → COUNTERINTUITIVE RESULT

Some questions are valuable because the expected result differs from reality.

Structure:

```text
EXPECTED
→ CONTRADICTION
→ ACTUAL RESULT
→ EXPLANATION
```

These are strong candidates for Curious Reality Shorts.

---

---

# 51. QUESTION VERIFICATION

A question may be marked VERIFIED when:

- the central factual answer is sufficiently understood;
- major claims are supported;
- major contradictions are investigated;
- meaningful uncertainty is documented.

VERIFIED does not mean:

"Everything about the subject is known."

It means:

"The content can now be responsibly developed."

---

# 52. QUESTION SCRIPT CONNECTION

When a script is created:

```text
QUESTION
→ PRODUCES
→ SCRIPT
```

The script should preserve the original question unless the research reveals that the framing needs correction.

---

# 53. QUESTION REFRAMING

A question may need to be reframed.

Example:

Initial:

Why does X never happen?

Research reveals:

X actually happens under specific conditions.

Better:

Why does X usually not happen?

The archive should preserve the original question but record the refined version.

---

# 54. QUESTION VERSIONING

Use:

```yaml
original_question:
refined_question:
final_question:
```

when a research process materially changes the framing.

Do not silently replace the original question.

---

# 55. QUESTION EXPANSION

A published question may generate:

- follow-up Shorts;
- long-form;
- articles;
- diagrams;
- comparisons;
- case studies;
- historical context;
- technical deep dives.

Record these expansions.

# 59. QUESTION CAPTURE PRINCIPLE

Capture first.

Evaluate later.

Do not require perfect formatting at the moment of discovery.

A good question lost from memory is more valuable than a perfectly formatted archive with no ideas.

---

# 60. QUESTION REVIEW

Review the archive periodically.

Ask:

Which questions remain unexplored?

Which questions became outdated?

Which questions became more interesting?

Which questions have new evidence?

Which questions belong to the same cluster?

Which questions can become follow-ups?

Which questions can form a long-form project?

Which questions are duplicates?

---

# 61. QUESTION GAP DETECTION

Look for:

- topics with many facts but few explanations;
- viewer questions without answers;
- concepts referenced but unexplained;
- mechanisms missing from the archive;
- follow-up questions not yet explored;
- interdisciplinary gaps.

These gaps represent content opportunities.

---

# 62. QUESTION PRIORITIZATION RULE

When several questions compete:

Prioritize the strongest combination of:

CURIOSITY
+
KNOWLEDGE PAYOFF
+
EVIDENCE
+
VISUAL POTENTIAL
+
RELEVANCE
+
ORIGINALITY

Do not prioritize only by search volume.

---

# 63. SEARCH VOLUME RULE

High search volume is useful information.

It is not enough by itself.

A high-volume question with weak explanatory value may be less valuable than a lower-volume question with:

- strong curiosity;
- strong payoff;
- excellent explanation;
- evergreen potential.

Search demand should inform selection, not control it.

---

# 64. TREND VS EVERGREEN BALANCE

The question archive should maintain a balance between:

EVERGREEN QUESTIONS

and:

TIMELY QUESTIONS

Evergreen builds long-term knowledge.

Timely content can capture current interest.

Neither should completely dominate the system.

---

# 65. QUESTION QUALITY OVER QUANTITY

Do not measure the archive only by the number of questions.

A high-quality archive contains:

- strong questions;
- meaningful clusters;
- useful relationships;
- research potential;
- knowledge gaps.

Ten excellent questions can be more valuable than hundreds of weak ones.

---

# 66. AI QUESTION GENERATION

AI may generate candidate questions from:

- existing topics;
- concepts;
- viewer comments;
- search behavior;
- research findings;
- content gaps;
- knowledge graph relationships;
- current developments;
- everyday observations.

Generated questions must then be screened.

AI-generated questions are candidates.

They are not automatically approved.

---

# 67. AI QUESTION DISCOVERY LOOP

Use:

SIGNAL
→ QUESTION
→ DUPLICATE CHECK
→ CURIOUS REALITY TEST
→ EVIDENCE TEST
→ VISUAL TEST
→ PRIORITY
→ ARCHIVE
→ RESEARCH

This should become a repeatable question-generation system.

---

# 68. AI QUESTION SAFETY

AI must not create a question around a fabricated premise and then treat the premise as true.

Example of bad workflow:

Invented claim
→ question
→ invented explanation

Instead:

Observed/verified premise
→ question
→ research
→ explanation

# 74. QUESTION ARCHIVE AS LONG-TERM ASSET

The Question Archive may eventually contain thousands of questions.

It becomes:

- idea reservoir;
- research queue;
- content strategy database;
- knowledge graph entry point;
- website discovery source;
- AI question-generation memory.

The archive should therefore be treated as strategic infrastructure.

---

# 75. QUESTION ARCHIVE MAINTENANCE

Regularly:

- merge duplicates;
- update statuses;
- refine questions;
- link completed videos;
- connect related questions;
- archive rejected ideas with reasons;
- update time-sensitive questions;
- identify knowledge gaps.

Do not let the archive become a dead list.

---

# 76. QUESTION ARCHIVE QUALITY TEST

A healthy Question Archive should allow the system to answer:

What should we research next?

Why is this question worth answering?

Has Curious Reality already covered it?

What topic does it belong to?

What related questions exist?

What evidence may support it?

Could it become a Short?

Could it become long-form?

What could it lead to afterward?

If the archive cannot answer these questions, its structure needs improvement.

---

# 77. GOLDEN QUESTION RULE

Do not ask:

"What topic should we post?"

Ask:

"What question is worth understanding?"

---

# 78. FINAL QUESTION PRINCIPLE

Curious Reality should never run out of ideas because reality itself contains more questions than the channel can publish.

The purpose of the Question Archive is to preserve those questions and turn curiosity into a structured, reusable production system.

---

# 79. CANONICAL QUESTION STATEMENT

The Curious Reality Question Archive is a permanent registry of questions worth investigating, explaining, visualizing, connecting, and preserving as part of the brand's long-term knowledge ecosystem.

---

# 80. ONE-LINE QUESTION RULE

Capture the question.

Protect the curiosity.

Then research the reality.
